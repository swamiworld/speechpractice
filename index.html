What this page is

A pacing aid for the two practices in Practice Material Book I — the Vocal Exercise and the Slow Reading practice in long lengthening style. Every word and sentence shown comes from your own copy of the book; the page only handles the timing, so your attention can stay on your breath, your speech organs and the sound you are making.

The book says to listen to CD‑1 to know the exact way each practice sounds. This page cannot demonstrate the style — it paces it. Let the CD be your reference for how it should sound, and use this to hold the rhythm and the clock.

Vocal Exercise — how the page paces it

  1. Every word in these exercises begins with a vowel. The coloured first letter is the sound you stretch.
  2. The word is spoken first, so you know what is coming.
  3. Breathe in while the circle grows. The book suggests inhaling through the mouth for a short initial period, then switching to the nose.
  4. Stretch the vowel as the bar fills, and hold it the whole length of the bar — Aaaaaaaa. On screen the vowel grows one letter at a time. No push, no force; throat, tongue, lips and jaw stay loose.
  5. Finish the word when the rest of it turns amber and the bar drains — …pple.
  6. Rest, and the next word arrives on its own.
  7. All three parts of the exercise are here — pick any lesson from the list: Vocal Exercise I (6 lessons, 80 words), II (7 lessons, 78 words) and III (9 lessons, 129 words). 287 words in all. Work through them for about 15 minutes. The four sliders below set the length of each stage.

Slow Reading — how the page paces it

  1. The book marks the text with slashes. Each slash is a stopping point: one phrase per breath.
  2. One phrase is shown large at a time. Read it aloud slowly, in the long lengthening style, while the bar runs.
  3. At the end of the phrase the pause cue appears — stop, breathe, and only then take the next phrase.
  4. Two sets of material: the 133 general instructions, and the Self‑Direction passage (the book suggests reading that one after dinner, before sleeping).
  5. If the auto pace does not suit you, switch to I tap myself and move with Space at your own speed. Your own rhythm matters more than the timer.

Practising with your eyes off the screen

  1. Put a headset on, stand at the mirror, and open Voice & cues below the practice area. Turn on Say each word and Beeps.
  2. Each word then arrives as sound alone: the word is spoken, then three rising tones walk you through it.
    Low tone — begin the in‑breath.
    Middle tone — begin the vowel and hold it, Aaaaaaaa.
    High tone — release, and finish the word: …pple.
  3. The three sliders set that shape: how long you stretch the vowel, how long you have to finish the word, and the rest before the next one.
  4. On screen the word shows the same thing, if you glance across: the vowel grows letter by letter while you hold it, then the rest of the word lights up amber when it is time to release.

Using the phone as your mirror

  1. The book asks you to practise in front of a mirror sometimes, to see that the speech organs are moving freely and to catch any unusual gesture. Switch Mirror on in Voice & mirror and the front camera fills a strip above the word, flipped so it behaves like a real mirror.
  2. Small / Medium / Large sets how much of the screen it takes — roughly a fifth, a third, or a little under half.
  3. The picture never leaves your phone. Nothing is recorded, nothing is uploaded, and switching the mirror off releases the camera straight away.
  4. It needs the https:// address. A copy of this page opened from your own disk cannot use the camera — browsers only allow it on a secure address.

The computer voice on the reading tab

  1. Speak with me reads each phrase in unison with you. Speak, then my turn reads it, then leaves you a gap to say it back yourself.
  2. It is a metronome with words, not a model. It cannot produce the long lengthening style — slowing it down only makes it sluggish. Take your sense of how it should sound from CD‑1, played in any music app, and let the computer voice do nothing but hold the pace.
  3. If your device has an Indian English voice it is offered first in the Voice list.

Deep breathing

  1. Sit straight on a chair, back straight, looking straight ahead at eye level. Rest your right hand on your stomach so you can feel it rise as you breathe in and fall as you breathe out.
  2. Breathe in through the nose while the circle grows and the count runs up to eight, then out while it shrinks and the count runs back down. Never hold the breath at either end.
  3. Correct breathing is noiseless — you should not be able to hear yourself. Keep the throat, chest and abdomen loose.
  4. The page counts the breaths for you, so both hands stay free and relaxed. The book has you counting to twenty on your left hand; there is no need to here.
  5. Avoid practising in a polluted place, or within two hours of a heavy meal.

Meditation — do this one from the book

Meditation is deliberately left out of this page. Its first stage asks you to close your eyes and count down mentally from 100, imagining the numbers written in front of you, or to picture a circle shrinking to a dot. A screen counting for you would be doing the very work the exercise is meant to make you do. The later stages — emptying the mind, then meditating on the points — are done with the eyes closed from memory, having read the points beforehand. Nothing on a screen helps with that. Read those pages, then put the book down and close your eyes.

Points the book keeps coming back to

  • Practise early in the morning, when it is quiet and the mind is fresh.
  • Regularity beats intensity. Irregularity even for a few days breaks the continuity of improvement — but excessive practice at a stretch for long hours can strain the vocal cords.
  • Practise in front of a mirror sometimes, to see that the speech organs are working freely and to catch any unusual gesture or movement.
  • Judge progress by how much you have improved, not by counting faltered words.
  • Speech sounds better in short phrases with regular pauses at the correct places — which is exactly what the slashes are training.
  • Keep the practices at the top of the priority list, and do not become careless once speech improves.
