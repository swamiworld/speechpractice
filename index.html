What this page is

A pacing aid for the two practices in Practice Material Book I — the Vocal Exercise and the Slow Reading practice in long lengthening style. Every word and sentence shown comes from your own copy of the book; the page only handles the timing, so your attention can stay on your breath, your speech organs and the sound you are making.

The book says to listen to CD‑1 to know the exact way each practice sounds. This page cannot demonstrate the style — it paces it. Let the CD be your reference for how it should sound, and use this to hold the rhythm and the clock.

Vocal Exercise — how the page paces it

  1. Every word in Vocal Exercise I begins with a vowel. The coloured first letter is the sound you stretch.
  2. Breathe in while the circle grows. The book suggests inhaling through the mouth for a short initial period, then switching to the nose.
  3. Speak as the bar fills: begin the vowel gently and hold it for the whole length of the bar, then finish the word — Aaaaaa‑pple. No push, no force; throat, tongue, lips and jaw stay loose.
  4. Rest, and the next word arrives on its own.
  5. Six lessons, 80 words. Work through them for about 15 minutes.

Slow Reading — how the page paces it

  1. The book marks the text with slashes. Each slash is a stopping point: one phrase per breath.
  2. One phrase is shown large at a time. Read it aloud slowly, in the long lengthening style, while the bar runs.
  3. At the end of the phrase the pause cue appears — stop, breathe, and only then take the next phrase.
  4. Two sets of material: the 133 general instructions, and the Self‑Direction passage (the book suggests reading that one after dinner, before sleeping).
  5. If the auto pace does not suit you, switch to I tap myself and move with Space at your own speed. Your own rhythm matters more than the timer.

Points the book keeps coming back to

  • Practise early in the morning, when it is quiet and the mind is fresh.
  • Regularity beats intensity. Irregularity even for a few days breaks the continuity of improvement — but excessive practice at a stretch for long hours can strain the vocal cords.
  • Practise in front of a mirror sometimes, to see that the speech organs are working freely and to catch any unusual gesture or movement.
  • Judge progress by how much you have improved, not by counting faltered words.
  • Speech sounds better in short phrases with regular pauses at the correct places — which is exactly what the slashes are training.
  • Keep the practices at the top of the priority list, and do not become careless once speech improves.
